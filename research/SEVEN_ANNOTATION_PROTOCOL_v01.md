## Purpose

This protocol tests whether the seven historical categories used by SEVEN can be distinguished reproducibly by independent readers.

It does **not** test whether exactly seven natural psychological structures exist.
It does **not** validate SEVEN as a psychological theory.
It does **not** treat fiction as empirical evidence about real people.

The first question is narrower:

> When independent readers examine the same short behavioural cases without being told the intended category, do they apply the seven labels in a sufficiently similar way to justify retaining them as a useful research vocabulary?

A negative result is informative.

## Research question

Primary question:

**Can independent coders distinguish the seven working categories with reproducible agreement, while still being allowed to mark overlap, no fit, or uncertainty?**

Secondary questions:

1. Which categories are most frequently confused?
2. Which cases attract multiple labels?
3. Does agreement change when context is added or removed?
4. Do coders repeatedly propose categories outside the historical seven?
5. Are some categories easier to identify from actions while others require inferred motive?

## Epistemic boundary

This is an **instrument pilot**.

The protocol is designed to test the usability and discriminability of the coding scheme before any larger empirical claim is attempted.

The pilot corpus may include synthetic cases and literary excerpts. Therefore:

- agreement in this pilot does not establish prevalence in real populations;
- agreement does not prove that the categories are natural kinds;
- disagreement does not by itself prove that the historical scheme is useless;
- fictional material is used to test the coding instrument, not to estimate human behaviour.

## Working categories

These are provisional operational descriptions for coding. They are deliberately broader than theological definitions and are subject to revision.

### 1. PRIDE

A motive or reaction organised around self-evaluation, dignity, superiority, status, recognition, humiliation, or protection of a valued self-image.

Do not code merely because a person has confidence or self-respect.

### 2. GREED

A motive organised around acquiring, retaining, accumulating, or recovering resources or advantage, especially where the boundary of “enough” becomes unstable or prior loss makes disengagement harder.

Do not code ordinary saving or rational acquisition without evidence that the acquisition/retention process is central to the case.

### 3. LUST

A motive organised around sexual or erotic desire, possession, access, pursuit, or gratification when that desire becomes central enough to alter judgment, boundaries, or treatment of another person.

Do not code attraction by itself.

### 4. ENVY

A motive organised around comparison with another person’s advantage, status, possession, relationship, recognition, or outcome, where the comparison becomes central to dissatisfaction or action.

Do not code admiration or aspiration without a comparison-driven negative component.

### 5. GLUTTONY

A motive organised around consumption, sensory reward, or repeated gratification beyond immediate need, where obtaining or repeating the reward becomes disproportionately important.

This category is not limited to food in the pilot; however, coders must not use it as a generic label for every repetitive pleasure-seeking behaviour.

### 6. WRATH

A motive organised around perceived violation, insult, threat, obstruction, or injustice that produces retaliatory, punitive, aggressive, or escalating action.

Do not code ordinary boundary-setting without a retaliatory or escalating component.

### 7. SLOTH / ACEDIA

A motive or state organised around disengagement, avoidance, refusal of effort, withdrawal from meaningful action, or resistance to confronting an unwanted reality when continued non-action itself becomes consequential.

Do not code fatigue, illness, disability, or lack of resources as moral failure.

## Coding unit

Each unit is a short behavioural case of approximately 60–180 words.

A unit should contain enough context to make motive interpretable but should avoid naming any of the seven categories.

Each unit receives independent coding from every rater.

## Coding form

For **each of the seven categories**, the coder assigns:

- 0 = not supported by the case
- 1 = plausibly present
- 2 = central to the case

The coder also answers:

- PRIMARY = one category that best explains the case, or NONE
- OTHER = yes/no
- UNCLEAR = yes/no
- CONFIDENCE = 1–5
- COMMENT = optional short explanation

Multiple categories may receive 1 or 2.

This avoids forcing every case into exactly one of seven boxes.

## Raters

Minimum pilot: **3 independent coders**
Preferred pilot: **5 independent coders**

Coders should:

- not participate in writing the specific cases they code;
- not be told the intended category of any case;
- not see other coders’ decisions before submitting;
- receive the same definitions and instructions;
- disclose prior familiarity with SEVEN.

At least one coder in the preferred pilot should have no prior involvement with SEVEN.

## Agreement measure

Primary reliability measure:

**Krippendorff’s alpha**, calculated separately for each of the seven 0/1/2 ordinal variables.

Reasons:
- works with more than two coders;
- supports ordinal coding;
- can tolerate missing ratings;
- evaluates each category separately, which fits a multi-label design better than forcing one seven-way choice.

Secondary descriptive measures:
- percentage of units with complete primary-label agreement;
- confusion matrix for PRIMARY choices;
- frequency of multi-label cases;
- frequency of OTHER and UNCLEAR;
- pairwise overlap of coded categories.

For the pilot, alpha values are interpreted cautiously. A commonly used convention treats α ≥ .800 as strong enough for drawing conclusions and .667–.800 as usable only for tentative interpretation; these are not magical universal thresholds and must be read alongside case-level disagreements and confidence intervals.

## What would count against the seven-category scheme?

The pilot should trigger revision if one or more of the following occurs:

1. Several categories repeatedly show low inter-coder reliability.
2. The same pairs of categories are confused across many unrelated cases.
3. OTHER is selected frequently and coders independently describe a similar missing category.
4. Coders agree on behaviour but systematically disagree on which of the seven labels should describe it.
5. Removing category names and asking coders to cluster cases produces a substantially different structure in a later phase.
6. Agreement appears only in cases written to strongly exemplify one category and collapses in mixed or naturalistic cases.

## What would support retaining the scheme?

Retention is supported provisionally if:

1. several categories show reproducible agreement across coders;
2. overlap patterns are interpretable rather than random;
3. mixed cases can be coded without forcing arbitrary single labels;
4. disagreements reveal boundary questions that can be stated explicitly;
5. coders who were not involved in SEVEN can still use the definitions with comparable reliability.

This would justify continued use of the seven labels as a research vocabulary. It would not prove that exactly seven natural psychological mechanisms exist.

## Pilot corpus v01

The first corpus is an **instrument-development corpus**, not an empirical sample.

It contains 28 units:

- 12 synthetic single-focus / control cases;
- 8 synthetic mixed-motive cases;
- 4 excerpts or paraphrased situations from the developing GREED manuscript;
- 4 externally sourced or public-domain behavioural cases added only after provenance review.

The synthetic cases exist to test whether the instructions are usable.
The GREED cases test whether the coding scheme survives longer-form motivational ambiguity.
The external cases are necessary before drawing any conclusion that the scheme works outside material generated inside SEVEN.

### Corpus construction rules

- Do not state the intended category inside the unit.
- Do not balance wording so obviously that coders can reverse-engineer the answer.
- Include at least six cases where more than one label is plausibly relevant.
- Include at least four cases where NONE or OTHER is a defensible answer.
- Include cases where similar outward behaviour arises from different motives.
- Include cases where similar motives produce different actions.
- Preserve provenance for every non-synthetic unit.
- Do not include private correspondence, clinical records, or identifiable personal material without an explicit ethical basis.

## Pilot procedure

1. Freeze protocol version and corpus version before coding begins.
2. Assign anonymous case IDs.
3. Randomize case order separately for each coder if practical.
4. Give every coder identical definitions and instructions.
5. Collect ratings independently.
6. Lock raw ratings before discussion.
7. Calculate agreement statistics.
8. Review disagreements qualitatively only after the quantitative pass is frozen.
9. Record proposed definition changes separately.
10. If definitions change, create a new protocol version and rerun a fresh or held-out corpus.

Do not edit definitions during a live coding round.

## Blinding

Coders may know that the project concerns seven historical vulnerabilities, but they should not be told:

- which category a case was designed around;
- the author’s preferred interpretation;
- the current common-root hypothesis;
- other coders’ choices.

For GREED excerpts, coders should receive only the minimum narrative context needed to interpret the unit.

## Exploitability as a cross-cutting dimension

Exploitability is **not** an eighth category.

For selected cases, a separate exploratory question may be asked:

> Given the motivational configuration coded above, what kind of promise, pressure, loss, reward, status cue, or opportunity would most plausibly alter the person’s next decision?

This exploratory field is qualitative in v01 and is not included in the main reliability score.

Its purpose is to examine whether different motivational configurations imply different points of leverage without redefining SEVEN as primarily a theory of manipulation.

## Relation to dynamic / network thinking

The dynamic-configuration hypothesis concerns relations among motives, not only their isolated intensity.

A later repeated-measures phase could present the same fictional or experimental subject across changing contexts and compare whether the pattern of relations among coded motives changes.

This is conceptually compatible with network approaches that model interacting elements rather than assuming one hidden cause, but SEVEN does not currently claim to be a validated psychological network model.

## Ethical boundary

This protocol must not be used to diagnose individuals, assign moral worth, or infer criminality.

For real-person material, future phases require:
- lawful and ethical data provenance;
- privacy protection;
- a justified consent or public-data basis;
- clear separation of behaviour coding from diagnosis;
- procedures for sensitive or harmful content.

## Invitation

The most useful external contribution at this stage is not agreement with SEVEN.

It is one of the following:

- critique the operational definitions;
- identify a known taxonomy that makes this test redundant;
- propose a stronger corpus;
- independently code a frozen pilot corpus;
- challenge the reliability design;
- show that the seven categories cannot be separated without circular reasoning.

A result that forces the scheme to change is a valid research result.
