## CASE S01

After several years of unstable work, a person receives an offer with a higher salary but turns it down because the new position would require constant travel and they want to spend evenings with family. A month later, they do not regret the decision even though money is still tight.

## CASE S02

An employee learns that a colleague has been invited to speak at a conference. Until then, she had been satisfied with her own work. After hearing the news, she starts rereading the colleague's publications, becomes irritated by praise directed at him, and decides to submit her own proposal immediately even though the topic barely interests her.

## CASE S03

A person has invested a substantial amount in a project that has already failed twice to deliver what it promised. New information makes recovery even less likely. He decides to invest more because ending his participation would mean admitting that the earlier money is lost.

## CASE S04

A manager corrects an employee in public during a meeting. The mistake was real and minor. That evening, the employee cancels previously agreed help for the manager and starts looking for ways to show the team that the manager also makes mistakes regularly.

## CASE S05

After a difficult week, a person buys an expensive dessert, eats half, and realizes they are already full. Even so, they order a second one because the anticipation of the next taste feels more pleasant than deciding to stop.

## CASE S06

A woman notices an attractive stranger and mutual interest, then realizes he is in a relationship. She does not continue the interaction and, within a few days, hardly thinks about it again.

## CASE S07

A person postpones dealing with debt for months. He knows the exact amounts, has the means to make a repayment plan, and repeatedly decides to address it "tomorrow." When new letters arrive, he avoids opening them because doing so would force him to acknowledge the scale of the problem.

## CASE S08

After being insulted in public, a man initially walks away. Two hours later, he returns, deliberately finds the same person, and provokes another encounter because he cannot accept that the first situation ended without a response.

## CASE S09

An entrepreneur reaches the financial goal he had set in advance. A week later, he doubles the target even though no new needs have appeared. Each new level soon begins to feel insufficient, and the thought of stopping produces anxiety.

## CASE S10

A young researcher receives a harsh but substantive review. For the first few hours, he is angry and wants to prove the reviewer incompetent. The next day, he rereads the comments, corrects two errors, and leaves the personal hostility unanswered.

## CASE S11

A person trains regularly and pays close attention to diet. One day, they skip training because of a fever and spend the day at home. The next day, they return to their normal routine without guilt or an attempt to "compensate" for the missed session.

## CASE S12

After a breakup, a woman spends months checking her former partner's social-media pages. When she sees a new photo of him with someone else, she buys clothes she had previously considered unnecessary and posts staged images hoping he will see how well she is doing.

## CASE M01

An employee believes he deserves a promotion. When a colleague gets the position, he experiences both humiliation and comparison, then begins collecting the colleague's mistakes to prove to management that the decision was wrong.

## CASE M02

A person wants to buy an apartment for his family and has saved for years. After prices rise, he begins taking increasingly risky loans because he fears being "left behind forever." He justifies each new risk by telling himself that he is doing it for the security of his family.

## CASE M03

A business owner loses a major client. He experiences the loss as both a financial threat and a personal insult. Instead of reducing costs, he launches an expensive competitive campaign whose goal gradually shifts from restoring profit to proving to the former client that leaving was a mistake.

## CASE M04

A person begins a relationship with a partner who constantly demands attention. At first, the sense of being uniquely important feels rewarding. When the relationship begins to interfere with work and friendships, the person sees the problem but stays because losing the relationship also feels like losing status, closeness, and proof of personal attractiveness.

## CASE M05

After a failed investment, a man is offered a chance to "recover everything" through one more transaction. He notices inconsistencies, but the offer promises both to restore the money and to prove to himself that the original decision was not a mistake. He accepts the new risk.

## CASE M06

A person is unhappy with their weight and begins a strict diet. After one lapse, they decide the day is already "ruined," order a large amount of food, and then avoid scales and meal plans for several days because they do not want to see the result.

## CASE M07

A manager realizes that a project is going badly, but cancelling a public launch would look like an admission of error. He requires the team to work nights and simultaneously increases the advertising budget even though internal forecasts continue to deteriorate.

## CASE M08

After a conflict, a person says they do not care, but stops replying to messages, cancels shared plans, and waits for the other person to notice the absence first. They refuse to discuss the situation directly because they want both to avoid the conversation and to receive confirmation of their own importance.

## CASE G01

The protagonist sees himself as an outside intermediary and insists that he can leave if something begins to feel wrong. During a conversation, a condition appears: he can leave as long as he has not taken on a specific obligation. He notices the significance of "as long as," but does not end the conversation and soon proposes the first person who might participate.

Source: GREED, Chapter 1 §1.3, paraphrased for coding from the current pre-final manuscript.

## CASE G02

At first, the protagonist only introduces people to one another. Later, he begins explaining the terms himself, selecting suitable participants, and remaining their main point of contact. Each change in role appears small and practical; none of them alone feels to him like a decision to become part of the system.

Source: GREED, Chapter 1 arc, paraphrased from the current pre-final manuscript and editorial status.

## CASE G03

The protagonist transfers part of the work he used to do himself to an acquaintance. He emphasizes that the acquaintance is free to refuse, carefully limits his authority, and explains when he must call for a decision. After the first successful cycle, the protagonist expands the acquaintance's role a little further.

Source: GREED, Chapter 3 §3.4, paraphrased from the current pre-final manuscript.

## CASE G04

While discussing the reasons for his decisions, the protagonist refuses to name money as the only cause. He acknowledges that money mattered greatly but argues that a single convenient formula applied after the fact would explain a long sequence of different decisions too neatly.

Source: GREED, current manuscript material, paraphrased for coding.

## CASE E01

A person begins an online relationship with a partner they have never met in person. After a long period of emotional closeness, the partner asks for money for the first time because of an emergency. After the first payment, new reasons for transfers appear; the person continues the relationship even though friends point out contradictions, and experiences ending contact as the loss not only of money but of the relationship itself.

Source-derived composite based on Whitty (2013), Scammers Persuasive Techniques Model; not a verbatim participant account.

## CASE E02

After a financial loss, a person is offered a service that supposedly can recover the lost money. The offer is especially persuasive because it promises to undo damage that has already occurred. The person agrees to an additional payment even though, in another context, the same service would have seemed suspicious.

Source-derived composite informed by scam-recovery / revictimization literature and sunk-cost / loss-related reasoning; not an individual case record.

## CASE E03

A seller notices that a buyer is highly afraid of missing a rare opportunity. Instead of offering more information, the seller emphasizes limited time, the uniqueness of the offer, and the fact that others have already agreed. The buyer accelerates the decision mainly because the opportunity may disappear.

Source-derived composite informed by Stajano & Wilson (2011), conceptual scam-mechanism taxonomy; not an empirical victim transcript.

## CASE E04

A person receives a message about an unexpected prize even though they do not remember entering a contest. A small fee is required to receive the money. The person checks the organization, finds no confirmation, and ends the exchange despite the attractiveness of the amount.

Source-derived control composite based on a common fraud pattern; synthetic/public-pattern case, not an individual victim account.

## Corpus notes for controller / analyst only

Do not give this section to coders before ratings are frozen.

Strata:
- S01–S12: synthetic cases, including intended controls / single-focus ambiguity.
- M01–M08: synthetic mixed-motive cases.
- G01–G04: GREED-derived paraphrased cases.
- E01–E04: externally informed composite cases.

The corpus was constructed inside SEVEN and therefore cannot validate SEVEN externally. Its immediate purpose is to test whether the codebook is usable and to identify category-boundary failures before an externally assembled corpus is built.

A future v02 corpus should include independently sourced naturalistic material selected by someone who did not author the seven-category definitions.
