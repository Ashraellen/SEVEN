## C01

A woman enjoys decorating her modest apartment until a sibling buys a larger home. She then starts describing her own place as embarrassing, replaces furniture she previously liked, and takes on debt for renovations mainly to avoid feeling inferior at family gatherings. Nothing about the apartment itself changed. The shift begins with comparison and the meaning she assigns to another person having more.

## C02

A musician has long compared himself with a successful peer. When the peer receives an award, he feels both resentment and anger at what he calls an unfair system. He begins posting hostile comments about the peer and contacting organizers with accusations he cannot substantiate. Comparison supplies the grievance, while retaliatory action gives the grievance a target. Either label alone captures only part of the sequence.

## C03

After dinner, a person feels physically full but keeps ordering increasingly elaborate desserts because the anticipation of another taste feels rewarding. The next course is no longer about hunger, celebration, or social pressure. He notices discomfort, says he should stop, and then orders again because ending the sequence feels disappointing. The repeated sensory reward becomes more important than the body’s signal that the need has already been met.

## C04

After losing money in a questionable scheme, a person is offered a paid recovery service. He notices inconsistencies but avoids checking them carefully because doing so might force him to accept that the original loss is irreversible. He pays again, partly to recover the money and partly to postpone confronting the finality of the loss. Acquisition and avoidance point toward the same next action.

## C05

An investor loses money on a highly visible recommendation he had defended to friends. A new high-risk opportunity promises not only to recover the loss but also to prove that his original judgment was sound. He invests again despite recognizing weak evidence. The prospect of recovering money and the wish to repair his image reinforce each other, making withdrawal feel like both a financial loss and a public admission of failure.

## C06

A manager is strongly attracted to a subordinate who has not encouraged personal contact. He begins assigning tasks that create private meetings, interprets ordinary politeness as hidden interest, and becomes irritated when she declines invitations. He knows the power imbalance makes pursuit inappropriate, but the desire for erotic access becomes a reason to reinterpret boundaries rather than respect them. Professional decisions increasingly serve the pursuit.

## C07

A person sells an investment at a profit large enough to meet the goal that motivated the purchase. Minutes later, he sees the price continue to rise and buys back in at a worse level because the additional gain now feels like money he is losing by standing aside. The original objective has already been achieved, but the visible possibility of more makes the completed gain feel inadequate and turns stopping into a perceived loss.

## C08

A man becomes intensely preoccupied with a new colleague he finds sexually attractive. He begins changing his schedule to create accidental encounters, ignores clear signs that she wants professional distance, and spends work time thinking about how to get her attention. He knows the pursuit could damage both careers, but treats each boundary as something to work around rather than a reason to stop. The erotic pursuit increasingly shapes choices that were previously unrelated to it.

## C09

A woman knows that several unopened letters concern an overdue administrative problem she can still resolve. She has the time, information, and ability to respond, but repeatedly moves the letters out of sight. Each delay makes the problem larger, which makes opening the next letter even harder. She spends hours on low-priority tasks to avoid beginning the one action that would force her to confront the situation she already understands.

## C10

A founder is offered enough money to sell a company and achieve the financial security he once described as his goal. He rejects the offer and pursues a much riskier expansion because the buyer would rename the product and his role would disappear from public view. The possibility of greater wealth matters, but so does the desire to remain identified with the company’s success. Money and legacy become inseparable.

## C11

A small business owner is content with steady income until a former classmate publicly announces rapid financial success. He begins chasing larger contracts outside his expertise, takes on debt, and talks constantly about catching up. Additional money now matters less as a practical resource than as evidence that he is no longer behind. Comparison drives acquisition, while acquisition gives the comparison a measurable score.

## C12

Two friends train for the same race. One is pleased with his progress until the other posts a faster time. He then changes his training plan, adds sessions his coach advised against, and becomes unable to enjoy personal improvements unless they reduce the gap. When his friend is injured, he feels a flash of relief before concern. His motivation has shifted from reaching his own goal to reacting to the other person’s advantage.

## C13

After a stressful day, a person begins ordering food and watching entertainment to avoid opening an email about a difficult but manageable problem. The eating continues after hunger is gone, and the viewing continues after interest fades. Each additional reward postpones the moment of confronting the email. Repeated consumption and avoidance support one another, making it hard to decide whether pleasure or escape is the more central mechanism.

## C14

A public speaker is mocked online after making a factual error. He corrects the error quickly, but continues reading comments and becomes determined to identify and humiliate the most visible critic. He spends hours preparing a response designed to damage the critic’s reputation. The factual issue is already resolved; retaliation now serves both anger and restoration of a threatened public image.

## C15

A collector owns several items that already satisfy the purpose for which the collection began. When a rare item appears, she borrows money to acquire it, then hides the debt from her partner. She explains that rarity itself makes passing impossible because the chance may never return. The collection has shifted from enjoyment and preservation toward acquisition as an end, even when another purchase damages goals she values more.

## C16

A professional receives an opportunity to apply for a role she has wanted for years. The application requires several hours of difficult self-presentation and carries a real possibility of rejection. She spends the week telling herself she is still considering whether the role suits her, but never opens the form. When the deadline passes, she feels relief before disappointment because no explicit rejection can now occur.

## C17

An artist receives a thoughtful review that praises the work but identifies a structural weakness. He initially agrees with the criticism in private. After learning that the reviewer is younger and less established, he decides the point is insulting and refuses to revise the piece. He tells friends that accepting the suggestion would make him look subordinate. The decision is driven less by the artistic question than by protecting a preferred image of his own rank.

## C18

A man receives medical advice to schedule a routine follow-up that is easy to arrange and not urgent. He keeps postponing the call, not because of cost or access, but because making the appointment would force him to think about a result he fears. He researches unrelated health topics instead and tells himself he is being cautious. The avoidance becomes a way of keeping an unwanted reality temporarily outside attention.

## C19

During a group discussion, a participant makes a dismissive joke about another person’s idea. The target initially responds calmly, but later begins interrupting the joker, dismissing unrelated suggestions, and looking for opportunities to embarrass him in front of the group. The original boundary could have been addressed directly. Instead, the response becomes a continuing effort to punish and restore balance through retaliation.

## C20

A student has fallen behind on a thesis but still has months before the deadline and access to supervision. He avoids opening the document because doing so would confront him with unfinished work and uncertainty. He spends long periods reorganizing folders, reading unrelated material, and planning elaborate future schedules. These activities create the feeling of preparation while allowing the central task to remain untouched and increasingly difficult to face.

## C21

A founder is offered a partnership that would solve a serious operational problem, but the other organization would receive public credit for part of the solution. Although the terms are favorable, he rejects them and chooses a more expensive path because he wants the project to be seen as entirely his achievement. The need to protect authorship and status outweighs the practical benefit he originally said mattered most.

## C22

An employee receives an unfairly rude email from a client. She drafts a sharp response, waits an hour, and still chooses language intended to humiliate the client rather than solve the problem. She copies additional people who do not need the information because she wants the client’s behavior publicly exposed. The response has moved from defending a boundary to making the other person suffer a comparable loss of face.

## C23

A person buys a new game and intends to play for one hour after work. He continues late into the night through repeated reward cycles even after the activity stops feeling especially enjoyable. He skips a planned meal, ignores fatigue, and starts another session because ending the sequence feels less appealing than one more reward. The case is about repeated consumption of stimulation rather than hunger, status, competition, or financial gain.

## C24

A neighbor repeatedly blocks part of a shared driveway. After several ignored requests, the affected resident has legitimate reason to set a firm boundary. Instead, he waits until the neighbor leaves and deliberately blocks the entire driveway overnight, even though this also inconveniences other residents. He says the point is to make the neighbor experience the same frustration. Retaliation becomes the goal beyond solving the original problem.

## C25

A person becomes intensely attracted to someone who has repeatedly declined romantic interest. He continues pursuing contact partly because of sexual desire and partly because he cannot tolerate the idea that he is someone who can simply be rejected. Each refusal increases both the pursuit and the need to reinterpret the other person’s behavior. Desire and wounded self-image become mutually reinforcing rather than remaining separate motives.

## C26

A driver is cut off in traffic and has to brake sharply. No collision occurs, but he follows the other car for several kilometers, ignores his own route, and waits for a chance to confront the driver. By the time he reaches them, the original danger has passed. He still wants the other person to feel fear and embarrassment because simply continuing his day would feel like allowing an insult to stand unanswered.

## C27

A collector subscribes to randomized digital reward packs in a game. He enjoys opening them, but also focuses on the market value of rare items. Even after spending more than planned, he continues because the next pack might deliver both the sensory excitement of opening and an item worth more than the cost. Repeated reward consumption and acquisition motives reinforce the same behavior.

## C28

Two adults begin a consensual sexual relationship and agree that neither wants exclusivity. One later becomes fixated on increasing the frequency and intensity of encounters, pressures the other after refusals, and treats access as something owed because of earlier consent. He cancels other responsibilities to pursue meetings and becomes angry when limits are restated. The issue is not sexuality itself, but desire overriding boundaries and unrelated commitments.

## C29

A researcher is satisfied with her own publication record until a peer receives a prestigious invitation. She starts monitoring the peer’s work, becomes irritated when others praise it, and loses interest in an article she had enjoyed writing. She submits to an event mainly because the peer will be there and imagines how satisfying it would be to outperform them publicly. Her dissatisfaction grows from comparison rather than from any change in her original goals.

## C30

A person uses short-form video to relax for a few minutes before bed. After an hour, he notices that he is no longer interested in most clips, yet keeps swiping because each next item offers a small possibility of reward. He delays sleep despite an important morning commitment and repeatedly says he will stop after one more clip. Continued consumption matters more than the content or any particular goal.

## C31

A senior employee is corrected during a meeting for a minor factual mistake. The correction is accurate, but he spends the rest of the day replaying the moment and imagining how colleagues now see him. That evening he drafts a long message listing the manager’s past errors and considers sending it to the whole team. His main concern is not the original mistake or its consequences, but the sense that his standing has been lowered in front of others.

## C32

An employee repeatedly compares herself with colleagues who appear to progress faster. Instead of applying for available training, she stops opening internal opportunity announcements and tells herself that advancement is probably reserved for people with better connections. Comparison produces discouragement, and avoidance prevents new evidence that might challenge the belief. The two mechanisms reinforce a stable pattern of non-action. Over time, the absence of action itself becomes evidence in her mind that progress is unavailable.

## C33

A woman becomes sexually interested in a person who is dating someone she knows. The attraction intensifies after she sees how admired the couple is socially. She starts comparing herself with the partner, imagines proving that she could attract the same person, and creates opportunities for private contact. Erotic desire and competitive comparison become increasingly difficult to separate. The imagined victory over the partner becomes part of what makes the attraction feel compelling.

## C34

A team leader privately realizes that a proposed strategy is failing. Several colleagues offer a simpler alternative and say they do not blame him for the original choice. He keeps the failing plan because changing direction would require saying publicly that others were right. Costs continue to rise, but he treats preserving authority and consistency as more important than correcting the decision while correction is still possible.

## C35

An employee receives a strong performance review and a raise. The next day, she learns that another employee received a slightly larger raise. Her satisfaction disappears, and she begins describing her own result as a failure even though nothing about it changed. She spends the week comparing titles, benefits, and praise, and decides to confront management mainly because the other person’s advantage has changed the meaning of her own outcome.

## C36

A woman in a committed relationship begins exchanging explicitly sexual messages with an acquaintance. She tells herself that nothing physical has happened and therefore no boundary has really been crossed. As the exchanges become more intense, she starts hiding notifications, inventing reasons to be alone, and taking risks that could expose both people. The attraction is no longer simply present; maintaining and escalating erotic access begins to reorganize her ordinary decisions.

## C37

A business owner has enough cash to meet all planned obligations and a stable personal income. When a speculative opportunity appears, he risks funds reserved for maintenance because the possible return would move him into a higher wealth bracket. After the first gain, he reinvests rather than restoring the reserve. Nothing in his living conditions requires the additional money, but stopping now feels like voluntarily giving up money that could still be captured.

## C38

A researcher avoids submitting a nearly finished paper. She says it needs more work, but repeated revisions focus on minor wording rather than substantive problems. Sending it would expose the work to judgment and possible rejection. Delay protects an image of what the paper could be while preventing any external test of that image. Avoidance and self-protection become intertwined over time.

## C39

A department head is contradicted sharply by a junior colleague in front of senior leadership. He believes part of the criticism is valid, but the public challenge feels humiliating. Over the next week, he excludes the colleague from meetings, rejects useful suggestions without discussion, and searches for procedural grounds to reduce the colleague’s responsibilities. Protecting status and retaliating against the perceived insult become difficult to separate.

## C40

A freelancer reaches the savings target she had set for a year of expenses. Instead of feeling secure, she immediately doubles the target without any new financial need. She begins accepting work she previously refused, cancels rest days, and checks her account repeatedly. Each increase feels reassuring for only a short time before the new amount seems insufficient. The thought of slowing down produces more anxiety than the prospect of exhaustion.

## C41

A person spends increasing amounts of time on sexualized online content while avoiding conversations about loneliness and a deteriorating relationship. The content provides immediate erotic stimulation and also postpones the discomfort of addressing problems outside the screen. Sessions grow longer even when satisfaction declines. Desire and avoidance appear together, and the case cannot be reduced confidently to only one of them without additional context.

## C42

At a tasting event, a guest is encouraged to sample many small portions. After the event formally ends, he continues moving between stalls and buying additional servings despite nausea and no remaining curiosity about the food. He says he wants the feeling of trying one more thing and becomes annoyed when a stall closes. The pattern is driven by continuation of sensory consumption after both need and enjoyment have substantially declined.
